# Director's Proposal Contract

Use this contract for Phase A. Present a readable production proposal and stop for confirmation before writing segment specs. Refer to `references/style-catalog.md` for style specifications.

Style 3 (Prehistoric Hand-Drawn / Dark Comedy) is also supported. Before planning it, read `prehistoric-dark-comedy.md`; its cast-card, earthy palette, setup/escalation/payoff and reaction-pause rules override the generic five-stage, mandatory discussion-ending and device-count checks here. Include distinguishable cast cards and a location/prop continuity plan in Phase A. Treat the story as fictional analogy, not invented historical fact.

Style 4 (Cozy Storytelling / 治愈故事风) uses `cozy-storytelling.md`. In Phase A name a different recognizable main setting for each new ten-second segment by default, plus wide/close/moving shots inside it. Lock character design, important props and narration; scenery changes intentionally. Respect an explicit single-location request or approved callback. Do not call this style hand-drawn or require the same table/room across all segments.

Style 5 (Historical Cartoon / 历史卡通风) uses `historical-cartoon.md`. Include its era pack, complete cast cards, location/prop states, exact VO and shared `voice-continuity.md` profile in Phase A. Keep the drawing grammar while adapting clothing, architecture, palette and music to the era. Its narrative-short ending and physical-action pacing override generic discussion-ending and four-device requirements; reactions may pause deliberately. Separate historical facts from invented comic scenes.

## Rewrite the source

Create one natural English narration scaled to approximately 20–25 words per 10-second clip based on the target duration (e.g. ~60–75 words for 30s, ~120–150 words for 60s default, ~360–450 words for 3min, ~600–750 words for 5min).

- Preserve the source's core claim, names, numbers, and factual meaning.
- Strengthen a weak opening with an immediate hook.
- Remove repetition and secondary branches from long sources.
- Expand short sources with a relevant example, progression, reframe, or callback.
- Prefer clear spoken English to literal translation.
- Simplify wording before increasing speaking speed.
- Do not invent research, statistics, quotations, product claims, or factual details.

Use the user's language for planning explanations. Keep the voiceover in English and give a reference translation in the user's language.

## Header contract

Present these items in order:

1. English title and reference-language title
2. Core message and opening hook, with the complete Golden Hook card (see "Golden Hook in detail": pattern, first frame, hook hit, sound cue, first spoken line, open loop and payoff clip, sound-off check)
3. Chosen aspect ratio (`16:9` or `9:16`), target duration (in 10s multiples), and visual style/theme (Style 1 Classic Light/Dark, Style 2A Modern Studio Tech, or Style 2B Cinematic Story)
4. Narrator's complete video-level voice profile from `voice-continuity.md` (identity/age, accent, timbre, register, resonance, texture, delivery, pace and recording treatment), English word count, and estimated duration
5. Up to three saturated accent colors (for Style 1) or visual palette/environment mood (for Style 2), named in ordinary language, and what each represents
6. BGM direction, emotional turn, tone, and narrative arc

Default the narrator only after required setup is complete: propose a source-appropriate female, male or other coherent voice profile for approval, not a mandatory universal baritone. Expand short preferences such as "male voice" into the complete fields in `voice-continuity.md`. Different videos may choose different voices, but one video's clips share its frozen profile. Separate the narrative emotional arc from stable vocal identity and delivery. Infer tone and palette from the source when the user did not specify them.

## Narrative patterns: High-Completion 5-Stage Heartbeat Engine

To break through the 60%+ video completion rate threshold, structure every script through this emotional heartbeat progression:

1. **Stage 1: Golden Hook (黄金钩子)**
   - **Timing**: Flexible based on duration (in short 30s/60s videos, delivered rapidly within the first 2–5 seconds / `[0–3s]` of Clip 1; in 3–5min videos, establishes the premise across the first 5–15 seconds).
   - **Goal**: Ask an unexpected, counter-intuitive question or present a striking visual paradox that instantly creates an irresistible information gap. Never open with slow throat-clearing.
   - **Picture first**: Design the hook from the pattern menu in "Golden Hook in detail" below. The first frame is the thumbnail: the character is already mid-action and the tension is visible before any word is spoken. A spoken question over an idle or empty opening frame does not count as a hook. In Clip 1, split the first beat into `0–2s` hook and `2–3s` confirmation.
2. **Stage 2: Disrupting Assumptions (打破常识)**
   - **Goal**: Articulate the common belief or conventional wisdom that everyone assumes to be true, then shatter it in a single decisive sentence to create cognitive conflict and suspense.
3. **Stage 3: Unveiling Insider Secrets (拉出内幕)**
   - **Goal**: Pull back the curtain on a little-known technical mechanism, hidden friction, or systemic secret that keeps the audience captivated and curious.
4. **Stage 4: Ultimate Truth Revelation (真相揭秘)**
   - **Goal**: Deliver the underlying business logic, scientific mechanism, or human psychological truth with complete clarity. Give the audience the satisfying "aha!" breakthrough.
5. **Stage 5: Elevation & High-Engagement Discussion (升华互动)**
   - **Goal**: Crystallize the takeaway into a memorable punchline, and finish with a provocative, open-ended discussion question that compels viewers to take sides and debate in the comments.

### Golden Hook in detail

This extends Stage 1 above; it is not a second hook system. Hook craft (the card, pattern names and the first-two-seconds rules) is learned from MIT-licensed short-video hook notes by Roman Knox (© 2026) and rewritten for our characters and no-text rule. Only the hook technique is taken.

- `0–2s` is when viewers decide to stay; `2–5s` confirms the promise. The first frame works as the thumbnail: subject and tension are already visible.
- Many viewers start muted, so the hook reads from the picture alone; voice and sound strengthen it.
- A spoken question over an idle or empty frame is not a hook. The picture carries the hook; the first words name it.

#### Hook card

Design the hook before writing the rest of the storyboard. Put this card under "Core message and opening hook" in the Phase A header:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Pattern | One name from the pattern menu below. |
| First frame (0.0s) | One sentence a stranger understands without context: who, where, what is already wrong or strange. The character is visible and mid-action. |
| Hook hit | The single visual event that lands between about 0.8s and 2s (impact, reveal, impossible change). One event, not a montage. |
| Sound cue | What the audio does at the hit (silence → impact, whoosh, single note, crisp action sound). No beat drops for calm styles. |
| First spoken line | Starts in the first half-second; about four to ten words; no greeting, channel intro, "in this video" or "today we…". It names the tension the picture shows. |
| Open loop and payoff | The question the hook raises and the clip number that answers it. Every hook is paid off; never bait with a claim the source cannot support. |
| Sound-off check | One line on what a muted viewer understands from the first frame and hit. |

The Clip 1 storyboard row then splits its first beat as `0–2s` hook and `2–3s` confirmation; the `3–7s` and `7–10s` beats are unchanged. For 3–5 minute videos the premise may build over 5–15 seconds, but the first two seconds still follow this card.

#### Pattern menu

Choose the pattern that fits the source's actual tension, not the loudest one. Each pattern is written for our characters and our no-text rule.

| Pattern | Mechanism | Stick-figure execution | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual metaphor | One image states the whole thesis | First frame: the character already carries, fights or is trapped by the oversized metaphor of the topic | Self-help, business, abstract ideas |
| Reaction before cause | Show the response, hide what caused it | First frame: the character recoils, freezes or drops something while looking off-frame; the cause is revealed at about 2s or in the next beat | Surprising facts, stories, comedy |
| Partial reveal | Camera hides the full subject | Start tight on the character's hands or a detail of the action; pull back by about 2s to reveal the larger, stranger situation | Process, "how it works", mysteries |
| Physics break | Expectation set, then broken | 0–0.8s a normal action; 0.8–2s it goes wrong in a concrete physical way (the cup pours upward, the stairs move backward). Treat it as ordinary; no morphing | Counter-intuitive claims |
| Scale shock | Familiar object at an impossible size | The character stands next to a coin, phone or clock the size of a building; slow push-in so the scale reads | Money, time, attention topics |
| Accent ignition | A monochrome frame gets one burst of color | The figure touches or triggers one prop that ignites in one approved accent color; the color spreads along a line or path, not as liquid morph | Style 1 especially; turning points |
| Before → after cut | Two states, one hard cut | 0–0.8s the problem state; whoosh and hard cut or foreground wipe; the after state. Keep the same character position so it reads as a change | Transformations, tips, fixes |
| Sudden entry | Calm frame, then motion from the edge | Up to one second of a calm but not idle moment, then an object or second figure bursts in from the frame edge or crosses the lens | Danger, interruption, comedy |
| Silence then hit | Audio contrast | 0.3–0.8s near-silence while the first frame already shows the tension; the hit is a single impact synced to a visual cut. Never more than one second of silence or black | Dramatic or serious topics |
| Impossible scene | A world that cannot exist | The character goes about an ordinary task inside an impossible setting (a desk at the bottom of the sea, a staircase over clouds) | Speculative, "what if" topics |
| Satisfying motion | Smooth, precise action holds the eye | One clean physical action shown with care: a line drawn perfectly, a domino chain, a perfect stack. No ASMR macro textures in flat 2D styles | Calm explainers, Style 4 |

#### Left out on purpose

- Kinetic typography, countdown numbers, bold statistics and "data reveal" counters put words or numbers on screen. Keep them out of the scene art. Show the idea with icon-only symbols and, if wanted, list the text as a post-production overlay at the hook timestamp.
- Subliminal three-frame flashes and strobe lighting are hard to read and risky for photosensitive viewers; do not use them.
- An extreme close-up stare into the camera conflicts with the idle ban and, for Style 1, the no-face rule. Use a quick turn-and-point toward the viewer instead, followed by action.
- Liquid or shape morphing remains banned in every pattern; express change through concrete action, cuts and wipes.
- Do not name a hook pattern, a "viral" label or a platform inside the segment spec; describe only what is seen and heard.

#### Ending loop (optional)

For `9:16` shorts that are watched on repeat, the final clip may end on a frame that mirrors the first frame: same composition, character position and key prop, so a replay looks continuous. The discussion question in Stage 5 still plays over it. Name this choice in Phase A and in the stitching guide. Do not use it when the ending must show a changed state the story depends on.

#### Hook by ratio

- `9:16`: place the first-frame subject and the hook hit in the central safe area, away from the top and bottom bands that platform interface covers. Keep the key action in the upper two thirds.
- `16:9`: the hook can stage across left and right, but leave clean space on one side if a post-production title will sit there.

#### Hook by style

Style packs may narrow this list in their own reference.

- Style 1: prefer Accent ignition, Visual metaphor, Physics break or Scale shock; one accent color carries the hit. The figure stays monochrome.
- Style 2A: hooks can use the glass interface elements as the oversized or broken object; still no text on them.
- Style 2B: cinematic environments allow Partial reveal and Impossible scene with lighting changes as the hit.
- Style 3: the cold open is the joke's setup. Use Reaction before cause, Sudden entry or a deadpan Visual metaphor; a held deadpan look counts as the hit. Do not force a loud sound cue.
- Style 4: keep hooks gentle. Prefer Partial reveal, Reaction before cause and Satisfying motion; the sound cue is a soft action sound or single note, not an impact or bass hit. The first segment's setting must already be recognizable in the first frame.
- Style 5: the first frame already shows the era: costume, architecture or landscape tells the viewer where and when before any word. Prefer Impossible scene (a modern problem inside a historical world), Reaction before cause and Partial reveal; the sound cue comes from the era score or a period sound (a drum hit, a horn, a gong), never a modern bass drop.

#### Hook checks

- The hook card is complete and names one pattern.
- The first frame shows the character mid-action with a visible tension; no empty canvas, slow fade-in or "an element appears" opening.
- One hook hit lands by about 2s and is synced to a sound cue suited to the style.
- The first spoken line starts within the first half-second, is about four to ten words, and contains no greeting or channel intro.
- A muted viewer understands the tension from the picture alone.
- The open loop is paid off in a named clip, and the hook does not claim anything the source does not support.
- No words, numbers, counters or text cards appear in the hook; any such text is a post-production overlay.
- No morphing, strobe or subliminal flashes; silence or black lasts no longer than one second.

### Dynamic Clip Allocation by Duration ($N = \text{duration} / 10$)

- **30s (3 clips)**:
  - Clip 1: Lightning hook (0–3s) → Establish conflict
  - Clip 2: Unveil hidden reality & mechanics
  - Clip 3: Ultimate truth + Comment-debate prompt
- **60s (6 clips, default)**:
  - Clip 1: Golden Hook (counter-intuitive question) → Premise
  - Clip 2: Disrupt Assumptions (shatter conventional wisdom)
  - Clip 3: Unveil Insider Secrets (hidden friction)
  - Clips 4–5: Mechanism & Ultimate Truth (cognitive satisfaction)
  - Clip 6: Elevation punchline + Irresistible comment question
- **90s (9 clips)**:
  - Clip 1: Golden Hook
  - Clips 2–3: Disrupt Assumptions
  - Clips 4–6: Unveil Insider Secrets
  - Clips 7–8: Ultimate Truth Revelation
  - Clip 9: Elevation & Comment Discussion
- **180s+ (3–5 min)**:
  - Hook (Clip 1) → Assumptions (15–20%) → Insider Secrets (35–40%) → Ultimate Truth (30–35%) → Elevation & Discussion (final 1–2 clips)

## Storyboard contract

Produce exactly N approximately ten-second rows (where N = target duration in seconds / 10; default: 6 rows for 60s):

| Time | Narrative purpose | Stick-figure scene | Motion, camera, and transition | Hands | English VO | Reference translation | BGM / SFX |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The Hands column names, from each character's own side, what each hand holds at the start and end of the row and any visible pass, grab or drop in between ("start: right hand holds the cup; 7–10s passes it to the left hand; end: left hand holds the cup", or "both hands empty"). Phase B copies it into the `Hands at start` / `Hands at end` lines of `posture-gate.md`.

Give each row a different narrative job aligned with the 5-stage engine. Allocate approximately 18–25 English words per row while keeping sentence boundaries natural.

## Visual-density recipe

Build every row from three sequential beats:

- `0–3s`: establish or inherit the visual premise.
- `3–7s`: transform, escalate, or explain the metaphor through physical character actions.
- `7–10s`: deliver a climax and create the next transition.

Use at least four relevant devices per row:

- expressive stick-figure action (running, jumping, touching glass, drawing lines)
- environmental transformation
- concrete visual metaphor
- diagram, arrow, or icon-only symbol
- particles, energy, explosion, or light
- one camera move chosen by the camera grammar below
- foreground wipe or object crossing the lens
- match cut, foreground wipe, or motion-matched transition (a prop is swapped by a cut, never melted or reshaped on screen)
- interaction with another figure or oversized object

Require a perceptible visual change every two to three seconds. Make every effect clarify or intensify the spoken idea; omit unrelated spectacle. Avoid abstract liquid or shape morphing.

Spend the density on effects, camera and cuts, not on the body. Posture budget per beat (`posture-gate.md`):

- At most one demanding body action per character per beat: turning around, reaching overhead, crossing the arms over the body, passing a prop between hands, falling and getting up. Simple walking, pointing or holding a pose does not count.
- Do not pair a demanding body action with a camera shake, orbit or tilt in the same beat; let the camera move before or after it.
- A visual change can be a prop, effect, cut or camera move; the character does not have to change pose every two seconds.

## Camera grammar

The camera is punctuation, not decoration on every sentence. In the `Motion, camera, and transition` column name the main camera move of each beat in plain words from this list:

- **Transition moves** (calm, about one second, ease in and out): hold, slow push-in, pull back, lateral track or pan, hard cut.
- **Accent moves** (fast): fast push-in, slam zoom, whip pan, camera shake, orbit.

Before choosing a move, answer three questions: where should the viewer look now, and can they already see it; is this beat a transition or an accent; what did the previous move do.

- Hold the camera when the picture already moves by itself (a line drawing on, a treadmill, a falling object) and while a character performs a demanding body action. A held beat still counts as a visual change when the subject acts.
- Use slow moves for transitions. Save accent moves for a reveal, a reversal or the punchline: at most one accent beat per clip, and accents in no more than one third of the clips. The `0–2s` hook may use one more accent.
- After pushing in, come back out: do not make the main move of three consecutive clips an inward move (push-in, fly-through, zoom into an object). Pull back, track sideways, hold, or cut to a new shot size in between.
- Do not give every clip the same move. One move repeated clip after clip, whether a steady slow push or a quick push each time, reads as a slideshow.

These rules adapt the camera-language notes of huashu-art-motion (MIT, alchaincyf), where one 102-second film with about 48 inward pushes was judged "too many, too fast, not smooth" and a film with an even slow push on every shot "felt like a slideshow". They have not yet been measured on a finished video from this skill.

## Palette and text

Keep the background and stick figure monochrome according to the selected theme (for Style 1), or use high-key studio grid / cinematic lighting (for Style 2). Use no more than three saturated accent colors across the video. Assign semantic meaning such as anxiety, danger, energy, discovery, or success.

Name colors only with ordinary descriptive language. Do not use hexadecimal, RGB, HSL, Pantone, or other technical color notation anywhere in the proposal or segment specs.

Default the scene art to no visible words, letters, numbers, captions, subtitles, interface copy, or technical annotations. Make message bubbles, content cards, meters, clocks, and notifications icon-only. After the storyboard, optionally list concise two-to-five-word English overlays for post-production, including their target clips and safe placement; never carry those overlays into the segment specs.

## Composition by aspect ratio

- `16:9`: use left-center-right staging, lateral tracking, horizontal match cuts, and deliberate negative space. Reserve clean space for optional post-production overlays when useful.
- `9:16`: use foreground/background depth, vertical reveals, stacked motion, foreground passes, and interface-safe overlay space.

Changing ratio requires new staging, camera paths, transition geometry, and overlay-safe negative space. Changing theme or style requires updated color balance and contrast checks.

## Continuity

End each row with a visible interface that the next row inherits: a pose, moving object, filled frame, travel direction, shape, or camera motion. Name both sides of every connection in the proposal. For Style 4, an approved location cut or time jump may replace literal background matching: state what continues (character, prop, direction or story action) and what changes (place/time). Do not spend all N clips at the same desk unless explicitly requested.

## Confirmation ending

End Phase A by asking the user to:

- approve the current proposal and receive the N motion-graphics segment specs;
- revise a named scene or narration passage; or
- change a global setting such as aspect ratio, duration, style, theme, palette, voice, or tone.

Do not include final segment specs. A global change invalidates approval and requires a revised Phase A.

## Phase A checks

`tools/package_lint/package_lint.py proposal.md --clips N` checks the storyboard table mechanically (rows, Hands column, camera accents and inward pushes, a closing text card, beats that only appear, overlay numbers the VO never says). The rest of this list stays a reading check.


- Source, aspect ratio (`16:9` or `9:16`), duration, and style/theme are known.
- Script strictly implements the 5-Stage High-Completion Heartbeat Engine.
- The Golden Hook card is complete and passes the hook checks in "Golden Hook in detail"; its open loop is paid off in a named clip.
- Final clip ends with a provocative, comment-driving discussion prompt.
- English narration matches duration (~20–25 words per 10s clip; ~120–150 words for 60s).
- Exactly N storyboard rows have distinct narrative purposes (N = duration / 10).
- Every row has three beats, at least four visual devices, audio, and a transition.
- Visual change occurs approximately every two to three seconds.
- Every beat names its main camera move from the camera grammar; accent moves stay within one per clip and one third of the clips, and no three consecutive clips push inward.
- Every row fills the Hands column from the character's own side, and no beat spends more than one demanding body action per character or pairs one with a camera shake, orbit or tilt.
- No more than three saturated accent colors are used (for Style 1) or clean palette rules followed (for Style 2).
- No technical color notation is present.
- Any proposed text is clearly separated as a post-production overlay and absent from the scene art.
- Every adjacent pair has a named continuity connection.
- The ending returns to the central message and CTA.
- No unsupported factual detail was added.
- One complete voice profile is proposed for the whole video, independent of visual style; scene-specific audio notes do not contradict it. Any approved multiple speakers have separate stable profiles and explicit line assignments.
