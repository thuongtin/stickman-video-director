# Embroidered linen: reference study

Read and visually inspected on 22 October 2026. These are three distinct historical objects, all from the Metropolitan Museum of Art. This is not three independent museum sources. The museum labels the images Public Domain. The repository contains no copies of those photographs or their motifs.

1. [Italian embroidered sampler, ca. 1600, object 20.186.366](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/221177). The full front photograph shows warm ivory ground, an orthogonal weave, fine ochre-brown thread outlines, unfinished motifs and quiet gaps. The museum records silk and linen on linen foundation with bobbin lace. We borrow the general construction lesson of a continuous woven backing and sparse versus dense stitch work, not the floral pots, ornaments, lace border or composition. Individual stitch types cannot be reliably identified from this overall photograph.
2. [Margret Barber sampler, 1661–63, object 57.122.368](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/228294). Full obverse image inspected. Muted olive, rust and blue embroidered bands contrast with pale whitework and a lower lattice. The official record names double-running, satin, eye, cross, Gobelin and reticella stitches. This supports studying directional parallel strands and short running-stitch paths. The visible relief does not establish padded embroidery; the overall image does not resolve microscopic fibre structure. No lettering, floral bands or striped composition is copied.
3. [Dutch sampler, 18th century, object 57.122.176](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/228103). Complete archival black-and-white image inspected. A woven grid supports isolated motifs with generous empty ground, compact filled shapes and thin lines. Colour is unknown from this image; “cross stitch” is not asserted because the source does not identify it. This supports consistent stitch scale and sparse placement. The figures, windmill, plants and layout are not copied.

## Synthesis, not a historical claim

The motion language is our proposal, not something demonstrated by the historical objects. The figure stays on one cloth plane, with small eased joint movement, a side listening tilt, open palms and a blink. No real textile, sewing mechanism, light transport or cloth dynamics has been simulated. The renderer approximates thread relief through thin shadows and highlights. It does not manufacture a physical embroidered item.

The original generic sprig is newly drawn from simple stems, leaves and buds. It is not a traced museum motif.

## Reference limitations

All three references come from the same museum and were studied as still photographs and records. The study supports a visual vocabulary, not claims of authenticity, historical attribution, audience demand or manufacturing feasibility. The image-generated concept is an original exploratory illustration, not evidence of real textile production.
